Cleaning interview audio with AI: Audacity and DeepFilterNet

How I connected Audacity to Claude to improve a nearly 22-minute interview, why the first version made it sound worse, and how a second pass with DeepFilterNet cut background noise by 21 dB without moving a single sample.

Matías Gaglio · 11/11/2026

Leer en español
The brief

A valuable interview with difficult audio.

I had a 21:46 interview recorded at 48 kHz, with background noise and heavy compression in the source file. The first 7:58 had to stay silent, as in the previous edit. The goal was a clear, steady voice without cutting, shifting or replacing anything in the existing project.

Connection

Audacity driven by Claude.

Audacity includes a scripting channel, mod-script-pipe, that can import files, apply effects and save projects through commands. I connected it to Claude Code through MCP. For analysis, Claude used Python with NumPy and FFmpeg to decode and compare files outside Audacity.

First rule: work from the original m4a rather than the already-edited mp3, because it carried one less layer of compression. Before touching anything, we confirmed that both matched sample by sample and found the exact moment the silence ended.
First pass

A classic chain that exposed the problem.

The first version used traditional effects: an 80 Hz high-pass filter, 10 dB of noise reduction, less body between 200 and 800 Hz, more clarity between 2 and 6 kHz, gentle compression, normalization to −16 LUFS and a limiter at −1 dB. On paper it worked: the gap between loud and quiet passages dropped from almost 13 dB to 7 dB.

Listening revealed dropouts and metallic highs. The diagnosis was specific: the noise gate opened and closed between words, and the overall gain also raised the hiss and compression artifacts of the 96 kbps file.

Second pass

Local AI instead of more effects.

The fix was DeepFilterNet, an open-source model built to separate speech from noise. It runs offline, so the audio never left the computer, and it works natively at 48 kHz. The alternative was a web service that requires uploading the recording to third-party servers.

The new project was saved under a different name and the previous one stayed untouched. I set that rule during the work, and it is now how I approach any automated process: never overwrite the source material.

Takeaways

What I learned.

  • More processing is not better sound: raising the level also raises the flaws that were already there.
  • Local AI protects the material: an interview can contain sensitive information and does not need to be uploaded to the cloud.
  • Numbers do not replace listening: metrics confirm nothing broke; ears decide whether it sounds natural.
