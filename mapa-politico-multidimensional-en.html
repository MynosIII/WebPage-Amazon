A multidimensional political map from 977 survey answers

A proof of concept that places each respondent on three axes (economy, society and institutions) and compares how people label themselves, what they answer and which leaders they identify with.

Matías Gaglio · 11/11/2026

Leer en español
Question

How people label themselves does not always match what they answer.

Political labels summarize a lot but hide differences. I built a proof of concept to study three things that do not necessarily line up: the identity each person declares, the positions they take on concrete questions and the leaders they identify with.

977responses analyzed
757distinct answer patterns
32interactive charts
13political identities compared
Method

From a form to a three-axis space.

Collect

Responses land in Google Sheets; a GitHub Actions workflow downloads them hourly as CSV.

Score

An audited key maps each option to a position on economy, society and institutional politics.

Analyze

Python with pandas, SciPy and scikit-learn computes associations, principal components and per-group dispersion in an isolated environment with no network access.

Publish

Plotly and JavaScript draw the charts; only aggregates and coordinates without personal identifiers are published.

Findings

A group's shape says as much as its center.

Besides where each identity sits on average, I measure how spread out its members are. A compact cloud shows agreement on the measured dimensions; a wide cloud gathers diverse positions under the same label. That describes the distribution and does not imply more openness or rigidity.

  • People with no declared identity are the most spread-out group: they do not form a bloc, and the missing label groups different phenomena that should not be merged.
  • The self-declared right is more heterogeneous than Peronism or social democracy on the measured dimensions.
  • The questions that best separate identities are about modes of production, markets and banks (bias-corrected Cramér’s V between 0.62 and 0.74).
  • The outlet people get their news from is strongly associated with the problem they rank first (V = 0.52).
Limits

What this analysis does not say.

  • Not representative: it is a convenience sample without population weighting; it describes this dataset, not the country.
  • Association is not causation: relationships are reported with effect sizes and a correction for multiple comparisons.
  • Small cells: some cross-tabs have low expected frequencies, so those values are read with caution.
  • Privacy: no table linking demographics to individual political answers is published.
