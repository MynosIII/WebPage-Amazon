Political social listening with open sources (OSINT)

How I built a daily social listening system for Argentine and Brazilian politics: which open sources it collects, how it classifies tone and narratives with Python and language models, and which privacy safeguards it applies.

Matías Gaglio · 11/11/2026

Leer en español
Context

Organize the public conversation before interpreting it.

Political information is abundant but scattered: articles from hundreds of outlets, video comments, search interest, fact-checks and social networks with very different dynamics. I built a system that collects those open sources every day, normalizes them and turns them into aggregate indicators for about forty politicians in Argentina and Brazil: mention volume, tone, dominant narratives, attention spikes and exposure to reputational risk terms.

The dashboard is a working tool and is not published on this site. What follows explains how it works and what kind of reading it supports.

Sources

Only open, free sources.

Each source has its own Python collector, with retries, rate limits and a status log for every run. If one source fails, the rest keep going and the dashboard reports it.

News

Google News and RSS

Searches per politician and per fact-checker, plus RSS feeds from outlets across the spectrum, each labeled with its editorial line.

Video

YouTube Data API

Public comments on recent political videos, by topic search and by channel.

Open networks

Bluesky, Mastodon and Reddit

Posts from public APIs that do not require privileged access.

Attention

Google Trends, GDELT and Wikipedia

Search interest, international coverage and page views for each politician as attention signals.

Explicit limit: social and news conversation shows what circulates and how it is framed, but it does not represent the whole population. That is why the system does not replace a survey with a proper sample design.
Method

From thousands of texts to signals you can explain.

Collect

GitHub Actions runs the pipeline daily and stores texts in a private cache, outside the public repository.

Classify

pysentimiento (RoBERTuito and BERTweet models on PyTorch) labels tone, emotion and aggression in Spanish and Portuguese. If the model is unavailable, auditable fallback lexicons take over.

Group

TF-IDF and NMF factorization in scikit-learn detect narratives: clusters of terms that appear together and how their share changes day by day.

Alert

Volume spikes by z-score, risk terms per politician and a simple 0 to 100 reputational pressure index.

The pressure index combines four components with visible weights: negative tone, aggression, risk terms and volume acceleration. I prefer a simple formula anyone can rebuild over an opaque score nobody can challenge.

Coordination

Spot near-identical messages without accusing anyone by mistake.

To flag possible coordinated activity, the system compares posts using character n-grams and cosine similarity. Two different accounts posting nearly identical texts (90% similarity or more) within an hour form an edge in a NetworkX graph.

A single match is not enough, because two people can copy the same headline. The same pair of accounts has to match on at least two different messages. In the period analyzed no group met that bar, and the dashboard says so instead of forcing a finding.

Privacy

Publish aggregates, never people.

  • Texts stay out of the repository: posts and comments live in a temporary workflow cache; only percentages and counts reach the repository.
  • Pseudonymized authors: accounts are replaced with hashed identifiers before analysis.
  • Legitimate sources: only public APIs and open data, with no access to private information.
  • Traceability: every indicator keeps the link to the public source behind it so it can be verified.
What it is for: spotting growing topics earlier, comparing how different politicians are discussed and giving a communications team verifiable context for its decisions.
